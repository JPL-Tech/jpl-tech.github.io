Best Budget Mesh Wi-Fi System 2026: What to Buy and What to Skip

A mesh system is a set of Wi-Fi units that work as one network. You place one unit near the modem and the others around the house, and the devices connect to whichever unit is closest. For a small flat, one unit can be enough. For a two-storey house with thick walls, a budget mesh kit often beats a single router that costs twice as much but still leaves dead zones upstairs.

The word "budget" needs a number. CNET's lab tests cover "mesh systems under $300" (CNET, consultado em 2026-10-14); we did not verify prices for multi-unit kits in the sources, and the sources do not give a price floor for the budget segment. Prices change often, so treat any figure here as a snapshot and check the current price on the day you buy.

What to check before you buy

1. Size of the home, counted in nodes. Most mesh kits are sold as one-, two- or three-packs. Testers usually recommend starting with a two-pack and adding a unit only if a room still has weak signal. Buying a three-pack for a small flat is wasted money. Buying a single unit for a large house forces you to add nodes later at a higher per-unit cost.

2. Wi-Fi 6 or Wi-Fi 7. Wi-Fi 7 is the newer standard and gives the highest peak speeds, but those speeds only matter if your internet plan is fast and you have devices that support the standard. For most homes on a plan of a few hundred megabits, a good Wi-Fi 6 system delivers what you will actually use. RTINGS describes the eero 6 as a dual-band Wi-Fi 6 system that handles internet plans of up to roughly 700 Mbps and "can handle demanding activities like multiple 4k video streams and high-speed downloads" (RTINGS, consultado em 2026-10-14). If your plan is above 1 Gbps, the Wi-Fi 7 picks are the safer choice.

3. Band support. Tri-band systems have a third band (6 GHz) that keeps the backhaul between nodes free of your devices' traffic. CNET notes that the Netgear Orbi 870 gets access to the 6 GHz band, which it calls "essential if you want to future-proof your home network" (CNET, consultado em 2026-10-14). On a budget, you can live without it; you should not expect it at the lowest prices.

4. Subscriptions and app control. Some mesh platforms lock features behind a monthly plan. RTINGS points out that ASUS "doesn't lock any of the ET9's features behind a monthly subscription" (RTINGS, consultado em 2026-10-14). Before you buy, check whether parental controls, security filters or advanced settings need a paid plan on the brand you choose.

5. Expandability. Check that you can add more units from the same brand later. RTINGS notes that the eero 6 lets you "add more eero units later if you need more coverage" and that the ASUS ET9 is compatible with other ASUS AiMesh routers (RTINGS, consultado em 2026-10-14). Expandability protects the price you paid for the first kit.

The budget picks, as the testers see them

Best budget mesh system: eero 6 (RTINGS). RTINGS names the eero 6 the best budget mesh Wi-Fi system it has tested. Its strengths, according to the review summary: compact nodes that are easy to place, dual-band Wi-Fi 6 that is enough for most ordinary households, and simple expansion. Its limit is range. RTINGS says it suits people who "don't need the range of our more expensive picks" (RTINGS, consultado em 2026-10-14).

Budget pick at the CNET lab: eero 7 (CNET). CNET writes that "of the mesh systems under $300 that we tested, the Eero 7 was in a class of its own" (CNET, consultado em 2026-10-14). The eero 7 is the newer step up from the eero 6 and is the one CNET lists as its best budget mesh system. Check the price on the day you buy: the gap between the eero 6 and the eero 7 is the number that decides whether the newer standard is worth paying for.

Upper mid-range: ASUS ZenWiFi ET9 (RTINGS). RTINGS describes it as "the best upper mid-range mesh system we've tested," a tri-band Wi-Fi 6E system with "notably low latency for a mesh setup" (RTINGS, consultado em 2026-10-14). It is not a budget system, but it is the next step if the eero kit does not cover your home.

Top of the market, for comparison: TP-Link Deco 7 Pro BE63 and Netgear Orbi 870. RTINGS names the Deco 7 Pro BE63 its top mesh pick, and CNET uses the Netgear Orbi 870 as its example of a tri-band system with 6 GHz support (CNET, consultado em 2026-10-14). We did not verify the Orbi 870 price in the CNET page, so this article gives no price for it. Read these as a benchmark for what you give up when you buy cheap, not as recommendations for a budget.

Which one to pick

  • Small flat, plan up to about 500 Mbps (our own rule of thumb, not from the sources): start with a two-pack. RTINGS names the eero 6 its best budget mesh system and says it suits people who "don't need the range of our more expensive picks". CNET's lab names the eero 7 the best of the mesh systems under $300 that it tested. The two reviews point to different models, so compare the price of the two on the day you buy and choose by that gap.
  • Larger house, or dead zones after the first install: CNET's lab pick (eero 7) is the newer step up. The sources we read do not compare the range of the two eero models directly, so test your own dead zones before paying the difference.
  • Multi-storey home, or you want to add nodes later: a three-pack from a platform with expansion options, such as the TP-Link Deco XE5300 that RTINGS lists as a three-pack for larger homes, or an ASUS system that mixes with AiMesh routers.
  • Fast plan above 1 Gbps: skip the budget kits. The Wi-Fi 7 picks are built for that speed.

Common mistakes

  • Buying more nodes than you need. Start with a two-pack and measure. Adding a unit costs less than returning a kit you did not need.
  • Placing the main node in a cupboard or behind the TV. Put it in an open spot, as high as practical, and near the modem.
  • Ignoring the modem. A mesh system cannot make a slow plan fast. Check the speed you pay for before you spend on the network.
  • Paying for 6 GHz when you do not need it. Tri-band is useful for large homes with many devices. For a small flat it is extra cost with little effect.

Next steps

  1. Measure your home: number of floors, distance from the modem to the farthest room, and your plan speed.
  2. Pick the category above that matches.
  3. Check the current price on the retailer the day you buy, and compare it with the price in this article.
  4. If you already have a router that works well in most rooms, try a range extender or a single extra node before you buy a full kit.
